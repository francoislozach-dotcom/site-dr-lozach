Conditions / Peripheral nerves

UNDERSTAND · LOCATE · TREAT

Nerve entrapmentin motion.

Hip, knee, lower leg, ankle and foot.

A nerve needs to glide as the body moves. A position, external pressure or muscle contraction can sometimes increase stress along its course. Understanding where, when and how symptoms occur helps guide assessment.

THE PRINCIPLE

Movement can reveal
an entrapment.

“Dynamic” describes entrapment affected by movement or position. Symptoms may be intermittent despite a reassuring resting examination. Some compressions are nevertheless persistent and only aggravated by exercise.

01

Sensation

Burning, tingling, electric pain or numbness.

02

Strength

Weakness depends on the nerve and injury level. Some nerves are purely sensory.

03

Context

A scar, injury or neuroma is not necessarily dynamic entrapment.

/01 — EXPLORE BY REGION

Hip & thighDifferent nerves, sometimes overlapping symptoms.

These landmarks help explain the anatomy. Pain location alone cannot establish a diagnosis.

Sciatic nerve · deep gluteal region

In the buttock and behind the thigh, muscular or fibrous structures may cause entrapment in the deep gluteal space.

Possible symptoms
Buttock pain radiating into the thigh or leg, sometimes affected by sitting or hip movement.
Keep in mind
A lumbar cause must be considered. Not every sciatic pain is piriformis syndrome.

Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve

Near the inguinal ligament; a tight belt or clothing may contribute to meralgia paraesthetica.

Possible symptoms
Burning or numbness over the anterolateral thigh.
Keep in mind
This nerve is sensory: an isolated lesion does not cause weakness.

Femoral & obturator nerves

The femoral nerve supplies the quadriceps; the obturator nerve supplies several thigh adductors.

Possible symptoms
Weak knee extension, or medial thigh pain with adductor weakness, depending on the nerve.
Keep in mind
These conditions are less common. Weakness requires investigation for proximal injury, haematoma or other causes rather than assuming exercise-related entrapment.

TARGETED NEUROLYSIS

Where can release be targeted?

  1. 01

    Sciatic · deep gluteal space

    Release may target tissues restricting the sciatic nerve in the deep gluteal region when this level explains the clinical findings. A lumbar cause must be distinguished.

  2. 02

    Lateral femoral cutaneous · inguinal region

    The passage near the inguinal ligament may be assessed for persistent, concordant meralgia. This sensory nerve does not cause motor weakness.

Femoral and obturator nerves: the target depends on the identified level and cause; there is no single release site for all thigh pain.

Educational landmarks, not incision plans or surgical indications on their own.

/02 — EXPLORE BY REGION

Knee & lower legSaphenous and common fibular: different nerves.

These landmarks help explain the anatomy. Pain location alone cannot establish a diagnosis.

Saphenous · adductor canal

A sensory branch of the femoral nerve, it passes through Hunter’s canal beneath sartorius before reaching the medial knee and leg.

Possible symptoms
Pain, burning or skin sensitivity around the medial knee or leg, sometimes extending to the medial foot.
Keep in mind
An isolated lesion does not cause paralysis. Infrapatellar branch pain in a scar is different from entrapment in the canal.

Common fibular · common peroneal nerve

It winds around the fibular neck on the outside of the knee, then divides into superficial and deep fibular branches.

Possible symptoms
Lateral or dorsal tingling, difficulty lifting the foot or toes, catching the toes or a slapping gait.
Keep in mind
SPE is the older French name for this same nerve. New foot drop needs prompt assessment for pressure, a brace, a cyst or injury.

Superficial fibular · fascial exit

This branch crosses the fibrous envelope of the leg to become subcutaneous. The exit level varies.

Possible symptoms
Burning over the lateral leg or dorsum of the foot, sometimes only during exercise.
Keep in mind
The first web space is usually spared. A muscle hernia may contribute, but its presence alone does not establish the cause of pain.

TARGETED NEUROLYSIS

Where can release be targeted?

  1. 01

    Saphenous · adductor canal

    The target may be Hunter’s canal. Infrapatellar branch involvement near the knee, particularly within a scar, requires separate localisation and a tailored procedure.

  2. 02

    Common fibular · fibular neck

    Neurolysis addresses restriction around the fibular neck and nerve entry into the fibular muscles. Assessment distinguishes this level from sciatic nerve or lumbar root disease.

  3. 03

    Superficial fibular · fascial exit

    Release may target the nerve’s passage through the leg fascia when symptoms and investigations identify this site. The exit level varies: the model marker is indicative.

/03 — EXPLORE BY REGION

Calf & ankleTwo tunnels. Two nerves.

These landmarks help explain the anatomy. Pain location alone cannot establish a diagnosis.

Tibial · soleal sling

In the proximal calf, the tibial nerve passes beneath the tendinous arch of soleus.

Possible symptoms
Calf pain with plantar sensory symptoms and sometimes weakness, depending on level and severity.
Keep in mind
This proximal location is different from the tarsal tunnel at the ankle.

Tibial · posterior tarsal tunnel

The nerve passes behind the medial malleolus under the flexor retinaculum, then continues into plantar branches.

Possible symptoms
Burning or numbness in the sole, sometimes worsened by walking or standing.
Keep in mind
The heel may be affected or spared because calcaneal branches vary. Posture, a mass or previous trauma may contribute.

Deep fibular · anterior tunnel

At the front of the ankle beneath the extensor retinaculum, or further along the dorsum of the foot.

Possible symptoms
Dorsal discomfort and tingling in the first web space; tight footwear may aggravate symptoms.
Keep in mind
Distal compression differs from common fibular injury at the knee. Ankle dorsiflexors supplied more proximally may remain intact.

Sural · lateral border

A sensory nerve of the posterolateral leg, ankle and lateral border of the foot.

Possible symptoms
Pain, burning or skin sensitivity, for example after a sprain, scarring or local pressure.
Keep in mind
An isolated lesion does not cause motor weakness. Its distribution and the history help distinguish it from tendon pain.

TARGETED NEUROLYSIS

Where can release be targeted?

  1. 01

    Tibial · soleal sling

    Documented compression in the proximal calf may lead to consideration of release beneath the soleal sling. This is distinct from tarsal tunnel release.

  2. 02

    Tibial · posterior tarsal tunnel

    The target lies behind the medial malleolus, with possible extension into plantar branch passages according to the location of entrapment.

  3. 03

    Deep fibular · anterior tunnel

    Anterior release concerns the passage beneath the extensor retinacula or an identified more distal entrapment on the dorsum of the foot.

Sural nerve: release may address a localised entrapment or scar adhesion. Injury or a neuroma may require a different strategy.

Educational landmarks, not incision plans or surgical indications on their own.

/04 — EXPLORE BY REGION

Sole & forefootSmall branches, localised symptoms.

These landmarks help explain the anatomy. Pain location alone cannot establish a diagnosis.

Medial & lateral plantar nerves

Tibial branches reaching the sole through fibrous passages including those beneath abductor hallucis.

Possible symptoms
Sole or toe sensory symptoms; the medial branch may be irritated in runners.
Keep in mind
Distal symptoms are not always explained by the tunnel behind the malleolus. Different causes may coexist.

Baxter’s nerve

The first branch of the lateral plantar nerve, implicated in some heel pain presentations.

Possible symptoms
Heel pain that may resemble plantar fasciopathy.
Keep in mind
Location alone is insufficient. Examination and, when appropriate, imaging help assess the possibilities.

Digital nerves · Morton’s neuroma

An interdigital nerve in the forefoot may become painful, often in the third interspace.

Possible symptoms
Burning, electric pain or a pebble sensation beneath the forefoot, sometimes affected by footwear.
Keep in mind
This “neuroma” is not an ordinary nerve tumour. Assessment and treatment are specific to this condition.

TARGETED NEUROLYSIS

Where can release be targeted?

  1. 01

    Plantar branches · passages beneath abductor hallucis

    Release may target a plantar branch and its fibrous passages. Baxter’s branch requires specific localisation and is not individually shown in this model.

  2. 02

    Forefoot · interdigital space

    For Morton’s neuroma, decompression or another procedure may be considered according to the assessment. Neurectomy differs from neurolysis: it removes a nerve segment and may leave numbness.

FROM SYMPTOM TO DIAGNOSIS

Connect the findings.
No single test tells the whole story.

  1. 01

    Describe

    Pain distribution, time to onset, provoking position and recovery time.

  2. 02

    Examine

    Sensation, strength, reflexes, gait, joints, spine and circulation. A single provocative test is not enough.

  3. 03

    Investigate

    According to the suspected cause: electrodiagnostic testing, possibly dynamic ultrasound, MRI or other targeted investigations.

  4. 04

    Bring findings together

    Symptoms, the suspected site and findings should be consistent before treatment is chosen.

Electrodiagnostics: location and severity+

Nerve conduction studies and needle EMG assess nerves and selected muscles. They can help distinguish a peripheral lesion from a more proximal cause. Normal findings do not exclude every intermittent or small-branch lesion, but do not prove “hidden” entrapment.

Ultrasound & MRI: looking for a cause+

Imaging may assess nerve calibre, scarring, a cyst, a mass or muscle changes. Dynamic ultrasound can examine certain relationships during movement. An abnormal image must be interpreted in the clinical context.

Targeted block: supporting information+

A local anaesthetic injection performed by a professional may help localise pain in selected cases. Temporary relief does not guarantee surgical success.

Exercise-related pain may also be muscular, tendinous, bony, vascular or spinal. Provocative tests, including scratch-collapse, are supporting findings and cannot decide surgery on their own. See references ↗

AN INDIVIDUAL STRATEGY

Treat the cause.
Not just the symptom.

MY PRACTICE

Neurolysis targeted to the identified entrapment.

I perform targeted neurolysis of lower limb nerves. The choice of nerve and release level is guided by identified sensory or motor deficits, symptoms and their agreement with clinical examination and relevant investigations. The aim is to free the nerve at the responsible site while preserving its continuity.

A deficit alone does not establish a surgical indication. Conversely, a purely sensory nerve may be affected without weakness. The cause, progression, previous treatment and potential for recovery are discussed in consultation. Release does not guarantee complete recovery, particularly in longstanding or severe injury.

01 / Reduce pressure

Adapt activity thoughtfully

Review footwear, equipment, pressure points and provoking movements. Rehabilitation and pain treatment are individualised. Avoid forceful stretching of an irritated nerve.

02 / Monitor progress

Track meaningful changes

Monitor walking tolerance, pain, sensation and strength. New or progressive weakness requires prompt reassessment. An ankle-foot orthosis may be prescribed to make walking safer.

03 / Consider a procedure

A clearly defined indication

Decompression may be considered for adequately documented, disabling entrapment despite appropriate care. A compressive mass or progressive deficit may change the priorities.

Decompression, neurolysis, neurectomy: what is the difference?+

Decompression opens a restrictive passage; neurolysis frees a nerve from surrounding restrictive tissues. Neurectomy divides or removes a nerve segment and may cause numbness or a new painful neuroma. These procedures are not interchangeable.

Injections and hydrodissection have varying indications and levels of evidence. Surgery may result in persistent pain, incomplete recovery, painful scarring, recurrence or nerve injury. Expected benefits and risks must be discussed individually.

SIGNS THAT NEED ATTENTION

New weakness?
Do not wait.

New or progressive weakness or foot drop, particularly after injury or surgery, needs prompt medical assessment.

In France, call 15 or 112 for a suddenly cold, pale foot, sudden severe pain with a tense leg, or back symptoms associated with bladder or bowel dysfunction or saddle numbness.

YOUR QUESTIONS

Clear answers, without shortcuts.

Are SPE and common fibular the same nerve?+

Yes. SPE is an older French abbreviation for the common fibular nerve, also called the common peroneal nerve.

Does a normal resting examination rule it out?+

It is part of the assessment, but may not capture an exercise-related problem. A clinician may use an appropriate movement assessment without deliberately provoking severe pain or weakness.

Is all exercise-related pain caused by nerves?+

No. Exertional compartment syndrome, tendon injury, stress fracture, vascular disease or a lumbar cause may produce similar symptoms. The appropriate investigations differ.

Does surgery mean a quick return to sport?+

There is no universal timeline. Return depends on the nerve, cause, injury and procedure. Releasing a nerve does not guarantee complete recovery of existing nerve damage.

Prepare for your consultation.

Note the exact area, triggering movement, time to onset and duration of symptoms. Mention any weakness, previous surgery or injury. Bring existing reports and images. Do not deliberately provoke weakness to demonstrate it.
